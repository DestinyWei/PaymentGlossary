Fund Flow

How money moves from payer to payee. This section mirrors the Chinese fund-flow chapter: collection, payout, clearing, settlement, netting, reconciliation, ledgers, pooled accounts, reserves, and treasury controls.

POBO payout
Example: group treasury pool
Subsidiaries in Germany, France, and Poland collect into a UK treasury account, then pay suppliers centrally. Benefits: lower bank fees, centralized FX hedging, and real-time visibility into group cash.
COBO + POBO — collection and payout on behalf of another party
COBO · Collection On Behalf Of
A platform collects money for a merchant, subsidiary, seller, or user. The payer sees the platform or a collection account, while the underlying economic owner is another party.
POBO · Payment On Behalf Of
A platform pays suppliers, sellers, creators, drivers, contractors, or group entities on behalf of another company.
Omnibus Account
A pooled bank or wallet account holding many users' funds together, while internal ledgers track each user's balance and movements.
Ledger Balance
The balance shown in the platform's internal books. It may differ from bank balance because of pending transactions, fees, reversals, and reserves.
Example: a marketplace collects customer funds into one bank account, records each seller's receivable internally, then pays sellers out later. In crypto, an exchange omnibus wallet follows the same logic: one chain address, many internal user balances.
Clearing vs Settlement vs Netting
Clearing
Calculating who owes what after authorization or transaction submission. It includes transaction data, fees, interchange, scheme fees, FX, and dispute adjustments.
Settlement
The actual movement of net funds between banks, schemes, processors, merchants, or wallets.
Netting
Offsetting payables and receivables so only the net amount is moved. This reduces bank transfers and liquidity needs.
Gross Settlement
Every transaction settles individually without offsetting. It is clearer, but more expensive and liquidity-heavy.
A card network may clear millions of transactions, net each participant's position, and settle only the final net amount. Stablecoin transfers often look like gross settlement because each on-chain transfer is visible individually.
Balances, reserves, and reconciliation
Available Balance
Funds the merchant can actually withdraw or use after pending transactions, reserves, and holds are excluded.
Pending Balance
Funds expected to settle but not yet available because clearing, risk review, or banking cut-offs are not complete.
Rolling Reserve
A percentage of merchant volume held back for a period to cover refunds, disputes, fraud, or future chargebacks.
Chargeback Reserve
A specific reserve against expected card disputes and chargeback liabilities.
Reconciliation
Matching transactions, invoices, bank statements, processor reports, refunds, fees, and settlements until every cent has an owner.
Settlement Report
A processor or bank file showing transactions, fees, adjustments, and the final payout amount.
Fund-flow operating controls
Safeguarding
Keeping customer funds separate from company operating funds, commonly required for e-money and payment institutions.
Float
Money temporarily held by a platform between collection and payout. It creates liquidity and compliance obligations.
Treasury
The function that manages cash, liquidity, bank accounts, FX exposure, reserves, and settlement timing.
Four-eye Approval
A control requiring two independent approvals before large payouts, account changes, or treasury movements.

Card Acquiring

The card-payment stack from checkout to authorization, capture, clearing, settlement, disputes, merchant payout, fees, authentication, and PCI scope.

A $100 online card payment, step by step
1
Card data enters checkout. The browser sends encrypted card data to the gateway.
2
Tokenization. The gateway replaces raw PAN with a token and routes the auth request.
3
Network routing. The acquirer sends the request through Visa/Mastercard to the issuer.
4
Issuer decision. The issuer checks balance, CVV, fraud signals, and places a hold. No final settlement yet.
5
Capture, clearing, settlement. The merchant captures after fulfillment; the network clears and settles net funds, usually T+1/T+2.

The five visible roles in a card payment
Cardholder
The buyer who owns and uses the card.
Merchant
The business selling goods or services and accepting the card payment.
Issuer
The bank or fintech that issued the card, holds the cardholder relationship, and approves or declines authorization.
Card Scheme / Network
Visa, Mastercard, Amex, UnionPay, and similar networks that set rules, route messages, and calculate fees.
Acquirer
The merchant-side financial institution that enables card acceptance and ultimately receives settlement for the merchant.
Processor
The technical operator handling authorization messages, clearing files, settlement files, and reporting.
Gateway, PSP, PayFac, ISO
Payment Gateway
The technical entry point that tokenizes, encrypts, and routes payment data from checkout to processor or acquirer.
PSP · Payment Service Provider
A merchant-facing platform that combines gateway, acquiring connectivity, payment methods, reporting, and operations.
PayFac · Payment Facilitator
A platform that onboards sub-merchants under a master merchant account, reducing onboarding friction but taking more risk.
ISO · Independent Sales Organization
A sales or distribution partner that refers merchants to an acquirer or processor.
Stripe and Adyen are often discussed as PSPs, but their exact role varies by region, merchant setup, and license stack.
Transaction lifecycle inside cards
Authorization / Auth
The issuer checks card validity, funds, risk, and permissions, then places a hold. No final money movement yet.
Capture
The merchant confirms that the authorized payment should be charged.
Clearing
Transaction details and fees are exchanged between acquirer, network, and issuer.
Settlement
Net funds move through the card network and banking rails.
Payout
The PSP or acquirer transfers available funds to the merchant bank account.
Void
Canceling an authorization before capture. The hold is released, and the transaction never becomes a charge.
Card fees and risk terms
MDR · Merchant Discount Rate
The total card acceptance cost paid by the merchant, usually shown as a percentage plus a fixed fee.
Interchange Fee
The fee paid to the issuer. It is often the largest component of card cost.
Scheme Fee
Fees charged by card networks for routing, assessment, cross-border processing, and other network services.
Acquirer Markup
The acquirer or PSP's commercial margin on top of interchange and scheme fees.
3DS / SCA
Authentication flows that ask the cardholder to prove identity, often shifting fraud liability.
PCI DSS
Security standards for handling cardholder data. Hosted checkout can reduce a merchant's PCI scope.
Disputes, chargebacks, and reversals
Refund
The merchant voluntarily sends funds back after settlement.
Chargeback
The cardholder disputes through the issuer, and funds may be pulled back from the merchant.
Representment
The merchant's response package arguing that the chargeback is invalid.
ARN · Acquirer Reference Number
A tracking identifier used to trace refunds and card settlements through the acquiring chain.

Invoice-led payments
Invoice
A document stating who pays whom, for what, how much, in what currency, and by when.
Invoicing
The end-to-end process of issuing, sending, collecting, matching, and closing an invoice.
Beneficiary
The party that ultimately receives the money.
Remitter
The party sending the payment.
Payment Terms
The agreed timing and conditions for payment, such as Net 30, due on receipt, or milestone-based payment.
Net 30 / Net 60
Payment must be made within 30 or 60 days after invoice receipt or issue date.
B2B finance operations
AP · Accounts Payable
The buyer-side workflow for bills owed to suppliers.
AR · Accounts Receivable
The seller-side workflow for invoices waiting to be collected.
Purchase Order / PO
A buyer-issued document confirming intent to purchase under agreed terms.
Goods Receipt
A record that goods or services were received, often needed before AP approves payment.
Three-way Match
Matching purchase order, invoice, and goods receipt before paying a supplier.
Remittance Advice
A notice explaining what invoice or invoices a payment is intended to settle.
Escrow and trade assurance
Escrow
A neutral holding arrangement where funds are released only after agreed conditions are met.
Milestone Payment
Payment released in stages as project milestones are completed.
Letter of Credit / LC
A bank guarantee used in international trade, promising payment if documents meet the agreed conditions.
Documentary Collection
A trade process where banks handle shipping and payment documents but do not guarantee payment.
Trade finance and supplier liquidity
Factoring
Selling receivables to a finance provider for earlier cash.
Invoice Discounting
Borrowing against receivables while keeping customer collection in-house.
Dynamic Discounting
The buyer pays earlier in exchange for a discount.
Supply Chain Finance
A program where suppliers get early payment based on the buyer's credit quality.

FX & Cross-Border

Cross-border money movement is a stack of messaging, bank accounts, FX conversion, compliance checks, cut-off times, local rails, and liquidity partners.

Bank wire
Costs can appear as transfer fees, intermediary deductions, FX spread, and delayed value date.
Stablecoin rail
The chain transfer can settle quickly, but on/off-ramp spread, liquidity, and compliance still determine final cost.
SWIFT and correspondent banking
SWIFT
A secure bank messaging network. It transmits instructions, not the money itself.
Correspondent Bank
A bank that holds accounts for another bank to support international payments.
Intermediary Bank
A bank in the middle of a transfer chain. It may deduct fees and add delay.
Nostro Account
Our account with your bank, typically in a foreign currency.
Vostro Account
Your account with our bank, from the receiving bank's perspective.
MT103
A SWIFT customer credit transfer message often used to trace international wires.
FX pricing
Mid-market Rate
The midpoint between buy and sell prices in the wholesale FX market.
Spread
The difference between the mid-market rate and the rate quoted to the customer.
Markup
The provider's commercial margin added into the FX quote.
Slippage
The difference between expected and executed rate, often caused by volatility or low liquidity.
Spot FX
A currency exchange for near-immediate settlement, commonly T+0 to T+2.
Forward FX
A contract locking in an exchange rate for a future date.
Settlement timing and rails
Cut-off Time
The daily deadline after which payment processing moves to the next business day.
Value Date
The date funds are considered settled and available.
ACH
A US batch bank transfer rail, often cheaper but slower than instant payment rails.
SEPA
Euro-area bank transfer schemes, including SEPA Credit Transfer and SEPA Instant.
FPS / Faster Payments
Local fast bank-transfer systems such as UK Faster Payments or Hong Kong FPS.
RTGS
Real-time gross settlement systems used for high-value bank payments.
Why cross-border card payments lose value
Cross-border Fee
A card network or acquirer fee applied when issuer, merchant, or settlement countries differ.
DCC · Dynamic Currency Conversion
Offering the cardholder a local-currency price at checkout, often with an embedded FX markup.
Withholding / Reserve
Funds held back for risk, refunds, and disputes, especially for cross-border merchants.
Beneficiary Deduction
Fees deducted along the transfer path before the final beneficiary receives funds.

Stablecoin payments, wallets, on-chain settlement, ramps, custody, token standards, chain selection, and crypto-native checkout flows.

Stablecoin primitives
Stablecoin
A token designed to maintain stable value, most often pegged to USD.
USDT
Tether's USD-pegged stablecoin, widely used for global crypto payments.
USDC
Circle's USD-pegged stablecoin, often used in regulated and institutional flows.
Reserve
The cash, bank deposits, T-bills, or other assets backing a stablecoin.
Mint
Issuing new tokens after reserve assets are received.
Burn / Redeem
Destroying tokens and returning fiat or reserve value to the holder.
Checkout, invoices, and payment links
Crypto Checkout
A checkout flow where the buyer pays with wallet funds or stablecoins rather than card rails.
Payment Link
A URL containing amount, asset, network, merchant, and payment instructions.
QR Payment
A wallet-scannable representation of the payment request.
On-chain Receipt
The transaction hash and chain data proving payment occurred.
Webhook
A server callback notifying the merchant system that a payment was detected or confirmed.
Confirmation Count
The number of blocks after a transaction, used to judge settlement confidence.
Wallet and network vocabulary
Wallet Address
A public blockchain address used to receive assets.
Private Key
The secret that controls asset movement. Whoever controls it controls the funds.
Seed Phrase
A human-readable backup for wallet keys.
Gas Fee
The network fee paid to execute a blockchain transaction.
Token Standard
Rules such as ERC-20, TRC-20, or SPL defining how tokens behave on a chain.
Bridge
Infrastructure for moving value or representations of value across chains.
Ramps and liquidity
On-ramp
Fiat into crypto or stablecoins.
Off-ramp
Crypto or stablecoins back into fiat bank money.
CEX
Centralized exchange. Easier onboarding and liquidity, but assets are typically custodial.
DEX
Decentralized exchange. On-chain swaps without a central order custodian.
OTC Desk
Over-the-counter trading desk for larger conversions and negotiated pricing.
Settlement Finality
The point after which the transfer is practically irreversible.

Crypto card operating model
Crypto Card
A card product where spending is funded by crypto or stablecoin balances, often converted before card settlement.
Prepaid Card
A card funded in advance from a stored balance rather than a credit line.
Debit Card
A card that draws from a deposit or wallet balance.
Virtual Card
A card number issued digitally for online spending.
Physical Card
A plastic or metal card used at POS terminals and ATMs.
Card Funding
The process that moves wallet value into the card spend account or available balance.
Program stack
BIN Sponsor
A licensed financial institution sponsoring the card program into a card network.
Issuer Processor
The processor that authorizes card spend, manages balances, and connects to networks.
Program Manager
The company orchestrating card UX, compliance, operations, vendors, and commercial rules.
Card Network
Visa, Mastercard, or another network handling acceptance and rules.
Settlement Account
The fiat account used to settle card network obligations.
Float Account
A funding account holding value available for card authorization.
Authorization and conversion
Spend Authorization
Real-time decision to approve or decline a card transaction based on balance, risk, compliance, and limits.
Pre-funding
Holding enough fiat or stablecoin liquidity in advance to support card settlement.
Just-in-time Funding
Moving or converting funds at the moment of authorization.
Crypto-to-Fiat Conversion
Converting stablecoins or crypto to fiat so the card network can settle in supported currencies.
FX Markup
Additional spread or fee when the card spend currency differs from the funding currency.
Velocity Limit
A risk rule limiting spend frequency or amount over a time window.

License Map

Payments are territorial. A company operating globally usually needs a stack of local licenses, registrations, exemptions, bank partners, and compliance programs.

World license map, simplified by operating region

United States and Canada

US federal money services business registration with FinCEN. It is not the same as state licensing.

State-by-state US licensing for money transmission.

Institutional Roles

Modern payment products are assembled from many institutional roles: licensed entities, bank partners, processors, networks, liquidity providers, compliance vendors, custodians, and program managers.

The party recorded as the seller in commerce and tax workflows.

A marketplace, SaaS product, or network coordinating merchants, buyers, and payments.
Sub-merchant
A merchant onboarded under a PayFac or platform structure.
Beneficiary
The recipient of funds.
Counterparty
The other side of a transaction or settlement obligation.
Infrastructure roles
Banking Partner
A bank providing accounts, safeguarding, payment rails, sponsorship, or settlement services.
Processor
The transaction-processing infrastructure layer for authorization, clearing, settlement, or reporting.
Network
A card, bank, or blockchain network setting rules and routing transactions.
Custodian
A licensed entity holding client assets or private keys.
Liquidity Provider
A partner supplying fiat, FX, crypto, or stablecoin liquidity.
Compliance Vendor
A provider for KYC, KYB, AML, sanctions, fraud, device, or blockchain analytics.
Risk ownership
Principal
The licensed entity ultimately responsible for a regulated activity.
Agent
An entity acting on behalf of a principal under permission or registration.
Outsourcing
Delegating operations to a vendor while retaining regulatory responsibility.
Material Outsourcing
A critical outsourced function that needs governance, monitoring, and sometimes regulatory notification.
